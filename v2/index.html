Two markets, scored separately

Owning and renting tell different stories.

A town can be cheap to rent and expensive to own, or the reverse. So each gets its own score, measured against county income rather than the town's own, so a wealthy town can't look affordable just because its residents are wealthy.

Owning

          How it works

          Public data in, plain answers out.

          1. 1

            Gather

            Every month the data refreshes from the Census Bureau, HUD, the NJ Department of Community Affairs and Freddie Mac, matched to towns by official ID, never by name.

          2. 2

            Measure

            Yearly housing costs are divided by county median income, then ranked across all towns as a 0–100 score with a confidence flag.

          3. 3

            Check

            Nothing is published until it passes validation: exactly 564 towns, no unexpected gaps, values in plausible ranges.

          Beyond price

          The barriers behind the numbers.

          Zoning rules and the state's affordable-housing obligations are shown alongside the scores, as context. They never change a town's score, and a town is never labelled as having "met" or "failed" its obligation.
