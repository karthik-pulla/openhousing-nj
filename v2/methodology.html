The question

For each of New Jersey's 564 municipalities: how much of a typical household's income would it take to own, or to rent, a typical home there?

We compare each town's housing costs with the median household income of its county, not of the town itself. If you divide a wealthy town's costs by its own residents' high incomes, it can look affordable precisely because only high earners can live there. Using county income asks the regional question, the same one New Jersey's Mount Laurel doctrine asks: could people from the surrounding region afford to live here? Town-income figures are shown on each profile too, labelled "display only".

Two scores, two markets

Owning and renting are largely separate markets, so there are two scores and no combined number:

  • Ownership affordability score: how the cost of owning the typical home compares with county income.
  • Rental affordability score: how the typical rent compares with county income.

Both measure prices relative to regional income. A town can rank well on one and poorly on the other; across New Jersey the two rankings are only loosely related.

Cost of owning

We estimate what it would cost each year to buy the typical home today with a standard mortgage, plus the town's property taxes:

Yearly ownership cost = 12 × monthly mortgage payment + average residential property tax bill

Ownership burden = yearly ownership cost ÷ county median household income

The monthly payment uses the standard fixed-rate formula: loan × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r is the annual rate ÷ 12 and n is the number of monthly payments.

Typical home value.

Property taxes come from the NJ Department of Community Affairs' Property Tax Tables: the average total tax (county, municipal and school) on a residential property, before credits such as ANCHOR or Senior Freeze. New Jersey's property taxes are among the highest in the country and are a large part of the cost of owning, which is why they're included.

Cost of renting

Rent burden = 12 × median gross rent ÷ county median household income

Median gross rent (rent plus utilities) comes from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey. Towns with very few rental homes may have no published median rent; they have no rental score rather than an invented one.

From numbers to scores

Each burden is turned into a percentile across all towns that have it: 100 means the smallest burden in the state (most affordable) and 0 the largest. The percentile is calculated as (rank − 0.5) ÷ number of towns × 100, with tied towns sharing their average rank. The rank (1st = most affordable) orders towns by that score.

Each score currently uses a single measure, so the score is simply that measure's percentile. The settings live in one configuration file, and this page reads them from the published data:

Context we show but don't score

  • Renters paying 30% or more of their income on rent. This describes how residents are doing, not what housing costs: it is high in places where renters have low incomes, even if rents are low. It is also very imprecise in small towns. It appears on each profile and as a map layer, clearly labelled as context.
  • A moderate-income household. For each county we use HUD's low-income limit (80% of area median income) for a four-person household and ask whether the typical home's ownership cost, and the median rent, would be 30% or less of that income. This ties the numbers to the households affordable-housing programs serve.
  • Burdens using the town's own income, for comparison with the county-based score.

Confidence flags

Every score carries a flag: high, medium, low, or insufficient data. Small towns are flagged, never dropped.

  • Survey precision. Census estimates come from a sample, with a published margin of error. We convert it to a coefficient of variation (margin ÷ 1.645 ÷ estimate).
  • Source. A home value from the Census is a five-year average of what owners think their home is worth, not a current market price, so it is at most "medium" confidence however precise it is.
  • Top-coding. The Census reports only that a value is "at least" a ceiling ($2,000,000 for home values, $3,500 for rent). Those towns are flagged low.
  • If a score has no usable measure at all, the town shows "insufficient data" and no score.

Each town's profile lists the specific reasons for its flags.

Barriers: zoning and obligations

These describe possible causes of unaffordability. They are shown as separate map layers and profile rows and are never part of either score.

Zoning restrictiveness

From the National Zoning and Land Use Database (NZLUD), compiled by Matthew Mleczko and Matthew Desmond (Eviction Lab, Princeton University) from municipal surveys in 2019-2022. Higher values mean more restrictive zoning. It covers only 105 of the 564 New Jersey municipalities; the rest are shown as "no data".

Fourth Round affordable housing obligations

Under the state's Affordable Housing Law (P.L.2024, c.2), the Department of Community Affairs calculated each municipality's obligation for 2025-2035 in October 2024:

  • Present need: existing deficient housing occupied by low- and moderate-income households.
  • Prospective need: the town's share of projected regional need, after the cap the state applied (shown in its table as the "1,000/20% Cap"). We also show it per 1,000 households, so towns of different sizes can be compared.

Qualified Urban Aid municipalities (47 towns) received, in the state's words, "an average allocation factor of 0 since they have no prospective need obligation under the Affordable Housing Law" (DCA report, p. 18). Their prospective need of 0 is a legal result, not missing data. They can still have a present need.

These are the state's calculated numbers. Municipalities could adopt different numbers through the law's dispute-resolution process, so a town's final obligation may differ. They are obligations, not records of homes built: OpenHousing has no construction data and never describes a town as having met or not met its obligation.

What this can't tell you

  • Small towns have large survey errors. Census estimates for towns with a few hundred residents can be far off; check the confidence flag.
  • Home values lag the market.
  • "Typical" isn't "every". Medians hide variation inside a town, and the typical home in one town may be a different kind of home from another's.
  • Average tax bills are for the average residential property, not exactly the typical home, and are before credits and deductions.
  • Costs left out: . Real ownership costs are higher than shown.
  • Second-home markets. Several shore towns rank as the least affordable to own. Their prices likely reflect second-home buyers from outside the county, so the comparison with county income may not describe what year-round residents face.
  • Zoning data covers only 105 towns, and obligations are calculated estimates, not built units.
  • Mutual-housing townships (for example Winfield and Audubon Park) have unusual home values and assessments; read them with care.

Sources

Every figure comes from a public source or is calculated from one. Missing data is shown as "Unavailable"; nothing is estimated, filled in or generated. The written summaries on each town profile come from fixed sentence templates filled with these numbers; no AI writes them. The full list of sources, with each one's data period, retrieval date and terms, is on the Sources page.

Downloading the data

The full dataset, the home value history and the data manifest are on the Data page.

The data refreshes automatically each month and is published only after passing validation checks: exactly 564 towns, no unexpected gaps, values in plausible ranges, and an unchanged format.
